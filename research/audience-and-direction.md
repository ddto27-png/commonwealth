# Commonwealth: who we are making it for

Working brief, September 21, 2026. Dana selected people making things as the primary audience, with curious collectors also welcome. The audience behaviors below are hypotheses to test, not established facts about our visitors. No visitor analytics or interviews have been conducted.

## The promise

A small, dependable collection of ideas that helps you make your next thing.

Give someone an unfamiliar reference, explain exactly how it works, and help them see a useful connection to their own project. Maintain clear boundaries between the maker's claims and our interpretation. The publication's value is selection, context and trust.

## Primary visitor: someone with a project in mind

Designers, creative technologists, artists, writers, students and independent founders exploring a project. Define the audience by what they are trying to do, not a presumed age, income or city. They may need a reference for a brief, a mechanism they can adapt, or evidence that an unfamiliar approach is possible.

Their useful journey: understand an idea in 20 seconds → inspect its mechanism and source in two minutes → save or share it for a real project. Those time budgets are design targets, not measured behavior. Each entry should answer: what is it, who made it, who does it serve, what actually exists today, how does it work, and what might it suggest for my own work?

## Secondary visitor: the curious collector

Someone who enjoys discoveries before knowing what they will make. Let them browse without needing a brief, save an idea, and follow a related thread. Avoid making every visit feel like homework. A short weekly edition and an accessible archive can serve both audiences.

## What to build next, in order

1. Trust: finish the source-to-copy audit and proofreading workflow. State prototype, waitlist and historical status plainly.
2. Useful references: add stable links to individual ideas, visible source dates and a simple way to share one entry. Keep the delightful tear animation, with a fast tap and reduced-motion path.
3. Project-oriented browsing: test filters such as preserving memories, gathering people, making sound visible or working with unusual materials. Pair them with concrete labels; retain broad browsing for collectors.
4. Return visits: publish four strong ideas per edition consistently, keep earlier editions accessible, and test whether readers want a weekly email. Do not add infinite scrolling just to increase time spent.
5. Places: keep historical references clearly separate from places someone can visit now. Only offer an actionable visitor guide when opening dates, venues and availability can be maintained.

## A two-week learning plan

Choose five makers with a current project and three curious collectors. Ask them to bring a recent brief or a topic they are exploring, then watch them use the site for ten minutes. Ask what they currently use, where they found their last useful reference, what they save, how they retrieve it, and whether they followed a source. Avoid leading with “would you use this?”

Record: time to understand one entry; any mistaken interpretation; whether a source was trusted; whether they could save and later retrieve an idea; one specific application to their work. Decide whether to invest in sharing, email or richer filters from these observations. Do not assume an age cohort or choose a geographic launch market without evidence.

## Business direction

Start as an independent editorial resource. After repeat use is demonstrated, explore paid thematic research packs or commissioned research for small creative teams. A membership could make sense if there is sustained demand for deeper research. These are options, not a monetization decision. Keep paid placements distinct from editorial judgment and disclose them if ever introduced.
